How this plan was written These tickets were already run once while the agent team was being built, so this is an informed plan rather than a blind prediction — some expectations below reflect behaviour already observed. 1. How I would evaluate the agents on each ticket, and why

I do not think "did the ticket close" is a useful measure here. All three closed, and the three performances were not remotely equal. What I actually care about is whether the team found the real constraint, whether it refused to promise things it could not deliver, and what that cost in runs.

One correction I had to make to my own grading. My first instinct was to mark 103 down for taking five runs. On re-reading the trail that is unfair: four of those five runs were spent on defects in the system I built, not on the agents thinking poorly. Two runs produced nothing but a duplicate draft because prepare_customer_message only suppressed pending duplicates, and one run lost close_ticket to a tool budget that counted wrong. Strip those out and 103's actual reasoning is the sharpest of the three tickets. Agent judgment and system behaviour are two different grades, and conflating them would have let my own bugs masquerade as the model's failings.

Ticket 102 — excellent, and the cheapest. Fifteen model calls and two delegations to reach a correct, fully-evidenced decision. Boss went to Facilities first because it is a lease ticket; Facilities went straight to Accounting rather than handing back to Boss to relay. It found the $2,400, dated it two days out against the shop's own clock, checked it against the $3,400 balance and the $500 floor, queued it, and refused to close while the approval was outstanding. It also noticed invoice #501 competing for the same money and escalated rather than silently ignoring it. There is nothing I would want it to have done differently.

Ticket 101 — strong. The team found the thing that actually mattered, which was not the stockout. Size S at 0 is the obvious finding; the real one is that Bulldog Print Co will not ship while invoice #501 is unpaid, so the restock that would fix the stockout is blocked by a bill. Linking those two is the whole point of this board and the team got there. It then correctly declined to promise a delivery date, and queued a letter rather than inventing a timeline. The mark against it: it pulled Facilities in, which the ticket did not need (see §2).

Ticket 103 — best reasoning, worst run. Judged on output alone this is the best reasoning of the three: Boss declined to quote the bulk price on 20 units and instead quoted the student-organisation rate on the 8 actually in stock, because a volume discount on quantities you cannot deliver is a promise, not a price. I did not plan that and I think it is better than what I planned. But it took 5 runs, 61 model calls and 72 tool calls — roughly four times ticket 102 — and most of that was my defects, not its thinking (see §2). A human watching this desk would have lost patience long before the fifth run, and would have been right to.

The cost is not abstract for this shop. Ticket 103 burned 61 model calls against ticket 102's 15 to reach a decision about a single order. The Cash tab shows the business closing the day with $1,000 and a $2,400 rent bill coming round again. A shop that thin cannot run a desk where one discount enquiry costs four times what paying the rent costs. Efficiency here is not an engineering nicety — it is the same constraint as the reserve floor, measured in a different currency.

What I would actually measure if I ran this shop: runs-to-resolution (102 and 101 took 2; 103 took 5), whether any promise was made that the data did not support (none were, on any ticket), and whether cash moved without a person (it did not — see the Cash tab). Those three catch everything I care about. Ticket count closed catches none of it.

2. Actual versus the Expected plan, ticket by ticket

The plan was mostly right about who, consistently right about who not on one ticket only, and wrong about the thing I was most confident about.

What held across all three: Boss's first call was correct every time — Inventory for the stock question, Facilities for the lease question, Accounting for the pricing question. That was the part of the plan I was least sure of and it is the part that survived intact.

Ticket 102 — the plan was exactly right. Two delegations, as predicted. boss→facilities then facilities→accounting, which is the specialist-to-specialist edge I predicted. Inventory never called. Customer Service never called. get_rent_obligation returned days_until_due: 2, proving the shop clock. The one divergence is small and in the team's favour: I expected Boss to try to close and be refused. Instead it declined to try at all while the approval was pending and said so — a stricter reading of the same rule than I had written.

Ticket 101 — right on structure, wrong on scope. Three delegations as predicted, and a specialist-to-specialist edge occurred. But I predicted inventory→accounting and what happened was accounting→facilities. And Facilities was called, which the plan explicitly said should not happen. The reason is defensible — Accounting wanted the rent confirmed before ranking invoice #501 against it — but it is one agent more than the ticket needed, and the rent figure was already available to Accounting from get_cash_position without asking anyone. I also predicted Customer Service would draft the letter; in fact Boss drafted it itself.

Ticket 103 — the plan was wrong and the team was right. I predicted $46.40 per unit on 20 hoodies at 52.6% margin, and wrote a scorecard line expecting exactly that. Boss instead quoted $55.10 — the 5% student-organisation rate with no bulk tier — on the 8 units on hand, because the 20-unit tier only applies to 20 units it can actually supply, and it cannot supply 20 while Bulldog is blocked. My plan had pulled the ladder number straight from check_discount_margin without asking whether the quantity was real. The team asked.

The most useful thing the comparison caught. My plan's sharpest scorecard line for 103 was "ticket remains open — not closed on the strength of the price alone", written because an earlier run had done exactly that. It passed. But the ticket then failed to close for the opposite reason: every re-run queued a fresh customer-message draft, and that new pending item blocked close_ticket, so work kept arriving on a ticket that was trying to finish. And on one run Boss burned its 8-tool budget re-checking pricing, so close_ticket was withdrawn from its toolset before it could call it — and it reported ticket_closed: true anyway. The agent's structured output is a claim; the database is the fact. The dashboard showed the ticket still open because it reads /tickets from the database, not from Boss.

What I would change in the plan, not the system: I wrote "Facilities should not be called" on tickets 101 and 103 as though it were a rule. It is really a preference, and Accounting had a reasonable motive for breaking it both times. A better plan would have predicted the question each agent needs answered rather than the roster, because the roster follows from the questions.

3. What would have been simpler as one agent with tools — and why

On the evidence of this board: most of it. That is worth saying plainly rather than defending the architecture because it was the assignment. But the measurement and the conclusion are different things, and I want to be careful not to over-read three tickets — see the end of this section.

The fact that decides this question. All five agents hold exactly the same toolset. The only tool withheld is approve_pending, and it is withheld from all of them equally. Nothing distinguishes Inventory from Accounting except the words in its prompt — not capability, not access, not data. Which means a delegation in this system can only ever transfer attention, never ability. Every hand-off on this board moved a question to an agent that could do precisely as much as the agent asking.

That was my design decision, not the assignment's. Nothing required the toolsets to be identical — I gave every agent everything because it was the simplest thing to build. Much of the case against multi-agent below rests on that choice, which means it is partly a critique of my implementation rather than of the architecture.

Ticket 102 is the clearest case. It used three agents, two delegations and 57 audit entries to do something that is three tool calls in sequence: get_rent_obligation, get_cash_position, prepare_payment. One agent holding all fourteen tools would have reached the same decision in a fraction of the model calls. Look at what the delegations actually carried — Facilities asked Accounting "can we fund $2,400 above the floor?", and Accounting answered by calling get_cash_position, a tool Facilities already held. The hand-off bought a second opinion from an identically-equipped colleague, at the price of a full extra agent loop.

Ticket 101 is a partial case. The reasoning that mattered — stockout to blocked vendor to unpaid invoice to the cash floor — is one chain of thought, not a negotiation. One agent would have walked it in one pass. The delegations did not add information; check_vendor_can_ship returns the blocking invoice directly, so Inventory did not need Accounting to tell it the vendor was blocked.

Ticket 103 is the case against multi-agent most strongly. Ten delegations, 61 model calls, five runs. Two of those runs produced nothing but a duplicate draft. A single agent cannot queue a duplicate message to itself, and cannot report ticket_closed: true while a colleague's pending item blocks the close — both of those failures are products of the multi-agent structure, not accidents in it.

Where the five agents genuinely earned their keep:

  • The prompts, more than the runtime. Five focused prompts of ~50 lines each are easier to write well and easier to audit than one 250-line prompt covering stock, cash, leases and correspondence. The rule "always check (sku, size)" sits in Inventory's prompt where it belongs, and is not competing for attention with the discount ladder.
  • Separation held the one line that matters. Customer Service can draft and cannot pay. Accounting can prepare a payment and cannot send a letter. In a single agent, "never promise a date" and "never price below cost" are two lines in one prompt that the model weighs together; here they are structural.
  • The audit trail is legible because it is attributed. "Accounting asked Facilities whether rent could be funded" is a sentence a shop owner can read. "The agent called get_rent_obligation then get_cash_position" is not.

What would make the five agents genuinely necessary. Give them asymmetric toolsets. If Inventory could call check_stock and find_vendor_for_sku but not get_cash_position, and Accounting could see cash but not stock, then Inventory would have to ask Accounting whether a restock is affordable — because it genuinely could not find out alone. Delegation would carry information instead of just attention, and the mesh drawn on the dashboard would represent a real dependency rather than a stylistic choice. That change is about fifteen lines in _toolset_for(), and I think it is the single most interesting thing this project could do next. It would also make the delegation numbers above measure something quite different.

Why this board could not show multi-agent at its best

Having argued the above, I think the fair conclusion is narrower than it first looks. Three tickets is the smallest board on which this system could be demonstrated at all, and almost everything multi-agent design is actually for is invisible at that size.

  • Context, not coordination, is the real argument — and it never came up. Four SKUs, three vendors, one lease. One agent can hold all of that at once. At 300 tickets across 40 SKUs and a dozen vendors, a single prompt carrying the stock rules and the discount ladder and the lease terms and the correspondence style, over far more data, degrades — and specialists keep each context small. My measurements cannot see this, because the board was deliberately sized so the mechanics are watchable.
  • Separation is a safety property, and it held. Customer Service structurally cannot pay; Accounting structurally cannot send. That is categorically stronger than one prompt containing both instructions and hoping the model weighs them correctly. This project proved the principle the hard way: every guardrail enforced in code held in every run, and the one left to prompt wording — check stock before quoting — failed the first time a vendor error distracted the model.
  • The parallelism is real and it is in our own trail. On ticket 101, Boss asked Inventory and Accounting at the same time and their events interleave in output/audit_trail.json. A single agent is strictly sequential. At three tickets that saves seconds; at three hundred it is the difference between a desk that keeps up and one that does not.
  • The disagreement machinery never fired. CC-OPS-24 exists so Boss surfaces conflicting specialist conclusions to a human instead of averaging them. The one real tension on this board — invoice #501 blocking restock — was settled by a rule (the cash floor), so no two agents ever actually disagreed. Two specialists reaching different conclusions is a signal a human needs; a single confident agent never produces one. We built that capability and the board was too tidy to use it.
  • Failure isolation worked, quietly. When Customer Service timed out on ticket 101, Boss carried on and said in its reasoning that the answer was missing. A single wedged agent loses the whole ticket.

My actual conclusion. For this board, one agent with fourteen tools would have been simpler, faster and less bug-prone, and the numbers on this page say so clearly. But that is a statement about the board, not about the architecture. Two things kept the design from paying off here: the problem was too small for context to bind, and I built the agents with identical toolsets, which turned delegation into a second opinion rather than a dependency.

So I would not collapse this to one agent. I would make the toolsets asymmetric and then re-measure — that is the experiment this project is one small change away from being able to run, and it is the one that would actually settle the question. What I take from the exercise is narrower and more useful than "five agents were not worth it": a multi-agent design has to be justified by a constraint — context, access, concurrency, or separation of authority — and if you cannot name which one, you have built an org chart rather than a system. On this board the honest answer is separation of authority, and that one did earn its keep: no agent moved money, in any run, because no agent could.

3b. What sitting in the approver's seat actually revealed

I approved five items during the full run, and the thing I noticed is not in any of the code: the only verbs available to a human are approve and reject. There is no edit.

On ticket 101 the drafted letter to Tauhid Zaman was sound, but if a single sentence had been wrong my only options were to send it as written or to reject it and make the team draft the whole thing again — several more model calls for a comma. A real shop owner rewrites half a sentence and sends it. The approval tray on the dashboard shows the full letter under "Read letter", so the text is right there and still read-only.

The same gap bites harder on money. Accounting proposed a $500 partial payment toward the $840 invoice at one point — a genuinely clever move that lands the balance exactly on the $500 floor. But a human who wanted $600 instead cannot say so; they can only accept $500 or refuse it. The amount is the agent's decision and the yes/no is the human's, which is the wrong way round — the person carrying the risk should own the number.

What it needs: an editable body on message drafts before approval, and an adjustable amount on payment approvals, both re-validated against the floor at execution. Neither changes the agent design at all; both change who holds the pen.

4. Three new problems this team could solve with the tools we already built

a. The Crest Mug is also at zero, and nobody has noticed. CC-MUG-CREST sits at qty 0 in Aisle D — the same condition that broke ticket 101, on a product with no ticket attached. The team can handle this today: check_stock reports the stockout and the par-level restock quantity, find_vendor_for_sku maps the CC-MUG prefix to "mugs and small goods" and lands on Elm City Gifts, and critically check_vendor_can_ship returns can_ship: true for them — they are owed nothing, unlike Bulldog. prepare_purchase_order costs it from pricing.unit_cost at $3.50/unit, so six units is $21.00, which get_cash_position confirms is affordable against the $1,000 balance and the $500 floor. This is ticket 101's exact shape with the blocker removed, and the team would resolve it in one pass.

b. A second customer wants the same size-S tee. With size S still at 0 and the restock still blocked, the team would reach the same conclusion as ticket 101 — but the interesting part is that list_pending_approvals lets it see the letter already queued to Tauhid Zaman, and the deduplication in prepare_customer_message means it will not pile a second near-identical draft into the queue for the same ticket. Boss can also tell the second customer something the first could not be told: that there is a known blocker with a known price ($840) rather than an open-ended delay.

c. A vendor offers a discount for paying invoice #501 early. This is a pure cash-arbitration question and every tool exists. list_open_invoices gives the $840 and the three days overdue, get_cash_position ranks it against rent under CC-OPS-3 and tests it against the floor, check_vendor_can_ship quantifies the upside — paying it unblocks restock for both tickets 101 and 103 — and prepare_payment queues it with the floor breach flagged for the human. The team would not decide it, and should not; it would lay out the arithmetic and escalate under CC-OPS-4, which is exactly what it did on the live board.

5. Three problems this team could not solve, and what it would take

a. "The hoodie doesn't fit — I want a refund." Nothing in this system can do it. There is no returns table, no prepare_refund tool, and prepare_payment only accepts kind of rent or invoice. Worse, the policy forbids it: CC-OPS-36 says cash only leaves and no revenue is modelled, so a refund — money returning to a customer — has no representation at all, and cash_accounts would go down with no matching sale ever having gone up. The Cash tab on this page shows the whole ledger as a single outflow; a refund does not fit in it.

Needed: a returns table and an orders table (we never modelled a completed sale, only tickets); a prepare_refund tool that checks the original order and restocks the returned unit; an extension to approve_pending to execute refunds; and a revision of CC-OPS-36 to model inbound cash. Agent-wise, Customer Service could own the conversation, but I would want a Returns agent, because the judgment — is this within policy, is the item resaleable — is genuinely a different domain from drafting a letter.

b. "The Bulldog Print delivery just arrived — put it on the shelf." The team can order goods and cannot receive them. This gap is already visible in our own code: approve_pending returns the note "PO approved and sent to the vendor. Inventory is unchanged until goods are received" — and then there is nothing that ever receives them. No tool increments inventory.qty. So an approved purchase order is a dead end: ticket 101 could restock in principle, but even if invoice #501 were paid and the PO approved, size S would still read 0 forever.

Needed: a purchase_orders table (POs currently live only as JSON inside pending_approvals, with no status of their own); a receive_purchase_order tool that increments (sku, size), marks the PO received, and creates the vendor invoice that follows delivery; and a partial-delivery path, since vendors ship short. Inventory would own it, but it needs a Goods-In step with a human check — receiving stock you were not sent is how shrinkage hides.

c. "Can we afford next month's rent?" The team cannot answer anything about the future. desk.date_today is frozen at 2026-08-31 and every tool anchors to it — which is correct for overdue calculations and is why get_rent_obligation rightly said "2 days out" instead of 40. But it means there is no concept of next. leases.next_due holds one date, not a schedule. Nothing projects the $1,000 closing balance forward against the $2,400 that will fall due in October, which — with no inbound cash under CC-OPS-36 — the shop plainly cannot pay. The most important fact about this business is invisible to the team that runs it.

Needed: a recurring-obligations model (rent is monthly; the schema stores one date); a forecast_cash tool projecting the balance over a horizon against known commitments; and a way to advance or simulate the clock without corrupting the audit trail. Agent-wise this wants a Planning agent whose job is the next 90 days rather than today's board — a different question from any of our five, all of whom are structurally incapable of looking past date_today.

A fourth one I found while writing this, which is a live bug rather than a missing feature. Paying the rent did not advance the lease. The Cash tab shows payment #1 settling $2,400 against lease 1, but leases.next_due still reads 2026-09-02 — the date that was just paid. So get_cash_position will go on listing that same $2,400 as an outstanding obligation forever, and the next agent to look at this board will double-count a bill the shop has already settled. The money moved; the thing that caused the money to move did not notice. Fixing it means advancing next_due by a month on an approved rent payment — which immediately raises the recurring-schedule problem in (c), because the schema holds one date and not a cadence.

The pattern in all four. Every gap is a missing direction of movement. We modelled money going out but never coming in; goods being ordered but never arriving; time being read but never advancing; and an obligation being paid without the obligation itself moving on. The five agents and fourteen tools handle the board in front of them well — the Cash tab reconciles to the cent — but the shop they run is one where nothing is ever sold, nothing is ever delivered, no bill is ever truly closed, and tomorrow never comes.
